Here is your voyage.

Scandinavia in late August is a region catching its last good light before the long exhale of autumn — the afternoons still generous, the harbor water still clear, the cities running full programs before the shoulder season quietly claims them. This itinerary doesn't rush through the Baltic; it moves deliberately, from Stockholm's medieval islands to Oslo's polar memory, with enough time at each stop to let the place actually register. Ten nights is the right length for what this part of the world asks of you.

What connects these ports is not a theme so much as a sensibility — a northern European seriousness about craft, history, and the natural world, carried lightly enough that it never feels like homework. The Grandeur sails it well. What follows is what you're actually sailing toward.

Stockholm  ·  Days 1–2  ·  August 29–30

Stockholm doesn't present itself the way most European capitals do. It reveals itself in layers — each of its fourteen islands a different chapter, the light off the water changing register every hour, the 13th century and the 21st sharing the same cobblestone without apparent tension. Your excursion is anchored to the Vasa Museum, and that's exactly right: a 400-year-old warship raised from the harbor in 1961, virtually intact, still carrying the carved warrior figurines that were meant to intimidate other navies. It is the only fully preserved 17th-century warship in the world. You'll view it from multiple levels, from keel to stern, and the scale of the thing — the sheer implausibility of its survival — tends to settle over visitors slowly rather than all at once.

Beyond Vasa, your two Stockholm days reach into Gamla Stan — the medieval old town founded in 1252, ochre and amber lanes, the Nobel Museum, the Royal Palace still in active use — and out to Djurgården, the royal park island that holds Skansen, the world's oldest open-air museum, and is quiet and green and beautiful in late summer. One more thing worth noting: the observation deck atop Stockholm's City Hall closes after August. You arrive August 29th and 30th. That view — across the archipelago, over a city that doesn't spread so much as float — is available to you, and then it isn't.

Baltic Sea  ·  Day 3  ·  September 1

A day on the open Baltic after two full days ashore is not a gap — it's the voyage catching its breath. The sea here is old, amber-colored in the shallows, carrying freight from a dozen ports you can almost name by looking at the horizon. Regent has designed days at sea as part of the experience, not as the absence of one: the itinerary earns this pause, and what comes next more than justifies it.

Berlin & Warnemünde  ·  Days 4–5  ·  September 1–2

Warnemünde is a Baltic resort village — lighthouse, long white-sand beach, a canal lined with fishing boats — that happens to sit at the end of a train line leading to one of the most historically charged cities on earth. The rail journey from Warnemünde to Berlin is 2.5 to 3 hours through the north German agricultural plain: quiet, useful, resetting. Berlin is not a backdrop. Brandenburg Gate, the Holocaust Memorial, Checkpoint Charlie, the remnants of the Wall at Potsdamer Platz and along the East Side Gallery — the city carries its 20th-century history in public, without distance. The Pergamon Museum holds the Altar of Zeus and the Ishtar Gate, artifacts that shift your sense of scale entirely. Charlottenburg Palace offers the baroque counterweight: gilded interiors, immaculate gardens, a different century's idea of what power should look like.

Two days here is a rare gift for a Baltic itinerary — most voyages give you one. Your second day is committed to Rostock, and that's the right call. Rostock is Germany's only coastal member of the Historic Highlights of Germany association — a Hanseatic city that Berlin's fame has kept largely to itself. The Neuer Markt's pastel Renaissance merchant houses, the Rathaus, the Marienkirche with its 13th-century Brick Gothic vaulting and an astronomical clock from 1472 that still marks the liturgical calendar. Walk it slowly. Let the architecture do the work. These two days are deliberately different in pace, and both are earned.

Copenhagen  ·  Days 6–7  ·  September 3–4  ·  Overnight

Copenhagen is a city designed to make you feel like you've been living at the wrong tempo — bicycles everywhere, open water at every angle, a design culture that prioritizes human scale, a restaurant scene that treats dinner as a three-hour event and means it. The overnight call is an asset. Most cruise passengers see the canal and Nyhavn and call it done; you have evening time, which is when this city actually shows itself. Tivoli Gardens in early September is in its late-summer program — founded in 1843, running concerts and illuminated gardens, its restaurant terraces full and unhurried. Walt Disney visited before designing Disneyland. The comparison flatters Disneyland.

Your canal cruise covers Christianshavn, the Little Mermaid, Amalienborg Palace — four identical mansions arranged in an octagon around a cobblestone courtyard — and the Nyhavn waterfront, the 17th-century harbor district that has appeared on more Danish postcards than anything else in the country. The Two Kingdoms excursion takes you to Kronborg Castle, the Renaissance fortress on a narrow strait that Shakespeare borrowed for Hamlet, all salt air and cannon smoke and literary weight, and to Frederiksborg Palace — 300 rooms of Dutch Renaissance architecture reflected in its own lake. September here runs 18–20°C in the afternoon and drops to 11–13°C after dark, which means Tivoli at dusk, a light jacket against the canal air, and no real urgency to be anywhere before the evening.

At Sea — Passage North  ·  Day 8  ·  September 5

The Grandeur turns north and the light changes. Early September on this passage means something different than the same date in the Mediterranean: the sky runs longer, the horizon sits lower, and the ocean has a density to it that southern waters don't carry. This is the passage toward Norway, and it feels like one.

Kristiansand  ·  Day 9  ·  September 6  ·  7 AM–5 PM

Kristiansand earns its name as Norway's summer city by being the Norwegian coast at its most accessible — white-painted wooden houses of Posebyen facing a harbor that still smells like the sea, a 17th-century fortress at the harbor mouth with intact cannons and good views in morning light, a fish market running proper meals of smoked salmon and local shellfish that are harder to find done right once you're back in Dallas. It's the Norway most visitors miss because they were looking for fjords. The pier at Kai 10 lands you in the heart of it within minutes, and the city is small enough that exploring on foot after your organized walk is genuinely easy — the grid layout makes it difficult to get turned around. One insider note: Kristiansand Cathedral runs a free 30-minute organ recital at 11 AM. Not every port offers that.

Oslo  ·  Days 10–11  ·  September 7–8  ·  Disembarkation

Oslo rewards those who expect more of it. Your Hadeland excursion takes you an hour outside the city to Hadeland Glassverk — Norway's oldest continuously operating industrial company, founded in 1762. Glass has been worked on this site for 264 years. Watch a master glassblower take a gather from furnace to finished vessel in under five minutes: the heat visible from six feet away, the material responding to breath and rotation and nothing else, the whole thing done before you've entirely registered that it started. The autumn light at Randsfjorden in September is excellent. The grounds, the shops, the gallery, the restaurants — plan to linger.

The Fram Museum at Bygdøy is the right place to end a Nordic voyage. The Fram is the strongest wooden vessel ever built — it carried Nansen and Amundsen farther north and south than any ship before or since. What matters is this: it is not a replica. It is the original ship, in original condition, and you board it. The Gjøa, the ship in which Amundsen navigated the Northwest Passage, is here as well. Stand on the Fram's deck and consider what it crossed. The voyage finds its weight here.

Ten nights from now, you'll disembark with a different understanding of what the North actually is — and what it takes to live well inside it.

One day on this voyage requires a bit of advance preparation, and it's Berlin. Your excursion departs the ship at 7:30 in the morning — the earliest departure of the entire trip. Regent's 24-hour room service is exactly what it's for, and you'll step off the ship rested and on time rather than scrambling. Berlin earns a full day of your attention — you want to arrive ready for it.

On specialty dining — you've already secured the three signature restaurants aboard: Pacific Rim on August 30th, Chartreuse on September 2nd, and Prime 7 on September 4th. If you haven't yet had a chance to browse the Culinary Arts Kitchen classes on the Regent portal, it's worth a look — they opened for booking in May and spaces for a six-person group are limited, but it's a genuinely fun shared experience if the timing works.

Copenhagen gives you two evenings, and that's a rare gift on a Baltic itinerary. The overnight stay frees up September 4th after your canal cruise and Tivoli excursion for an evening out in the city. If you'd like a genuinely excellent Copenhagen dinner, Höst (on Nørre Farimagsgade) is a warm, group-friendly Nordic restaurant that doesn't require a three-month reservation or a twelve-course commitment — just a genuine meal with beautiful seasonal ingredients and very good wine. Advance booking two to three weeks out is usually sufficient. Worth having on your list if you want to use the evening.

Your last evening in Oslo after Hadeland and the Fram is a natural occasion for something special. Statholdergaarden — in a 17th-century mansion at Rådhusgata 11 — handles groups of six well and offers private dining rooms that make the farewell dinner feel like a real event. Norwegian-French, exceptional service, the right setting for the end of a voyage like this. Advance booking a couple of weeks out typically secures it for early September.

One packing note worth the mention: the voyage is well-paced overall, with the more physically demanding days (Berlin, the Two Kingdoms excursion) built around recovery time (Rostock at 9:00, the at-sea day before Kristiansand). Plan comfortable walking shoes for Berlin and the Copenhagen castles — uneven surfaces on both. The Kristiansand walk is cobbled throughout Posebyen. Evenings in Copenhagen and Norway drop into the low teens Celsius — a good jacket pays for itself by September 4th.
