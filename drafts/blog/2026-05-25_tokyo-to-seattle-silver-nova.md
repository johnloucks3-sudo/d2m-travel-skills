# 32 Days, One Ocean, Zero Shortcuts: Tokyo to Seattle on the Silver Nova

There's a particular silence that happens somewhere around day five at sea. No horizon but water. The ship's engines a low, constant hum beneath you. The only thing that moves is the bow cutting west — or east, depending on which side you're watching from. That silence is why I do this.

This past April, my wife Susan and I spent 32 days traveling from Colorado Springs to Tokyo, across the North Pacific, and home to Seattle on Silversea's Silver Nova. Friends and colleagues who've heard about it tend to respond with some version of the same question: *How do you plan something like that?* The honest answer is: the same way you'd plan anything worth doing — one decision at a time, with enough patience to get the details right.

This is the story of how it went.

---

## The Build-Up: Orange County and Five Days in Waikiki

The trip started well before Tokyo. We flew to Orange County first — a family visit, a reset before the real travel began. Three nights at a Marriott in Newport Beach, time with our son Greg. The logistics of a 32-day international journey have their own gravitational pull. You spend weeks tracking confirmations, rebooking what the airline changed, monitoring the weather at ports you've never visited. Those three days in Orange County were the last stretch of genuine stillness before everything started moving.

Then Hawaii.

If you've never stopped in Honolulu on the way to Japan, I'd argue you're doing it wrong. The Hale Koa Hotel in Waikiki is a military resort — available to active duty, retirees, and veterans — and I can tell you it stands up to any civilian property on the island at a fraction of the price. Five nights, ocean view, and a stretch of the same beach that the major resorts are charging four times as much to access. We ate well, walked far, and watched the sun drop into the water every evening without once checking what time it was back home. That's a Honolulu calibration.

---

## Business Class to Tokyo: What JAL Gets Right

The Japan Airlines HNL–HND flight is one of the most underrated business class products in the Pacific. The Sky Suite III cabin is a full lie-flat private suite with a sliding privacy door and a window that faces you properly. No sliding past a stranger to get to your seat. The food is Japanese before it's airline food — a distinction that matters at 30,000 feet over the open ocean. We landed in Tokyo before dawn, cleared customs in forty minutes, and were in our room at the Hilton Odaiba before breakfast.

Tokyo gets covered extensively in travel media, and deservedly so. What I'll say is this: the city rewards specificity. A generic "Tokyo itinerary" misses the point. The morning we hired a guide named Hiro to take us through Kyoto's food stalls — the *nishiki* market, a handmade tofu shop that had been operating since the 1870s, a ramen counter that seated eight people and had a 45-minute wait — that morning was not available on any standard tour. You get Tokyo right by finding the people who know one neighborhood the way a local knows their own kitchen. That's the work.

Mt. Fuji the next day was a different energy entirely. The Hakone bus tour has its critics among the travel cognoscenti, and yes, on a clear day the mountain doesn't need a bus tour to reveal itself. But the day we went, the clouds parted long enough to show Fuji in the full silence of a volcanic morning, and the organized framework had us at the right viewpoint at the right hour. Sometimes the tour is right.

---

## Boarding the Silver Nova at Harumi Port

The embarkation at Tokyo Harumi is quieter than you'd expect for a ship of Silver Nova's scale. Silversea keeps its terminal operations unhurried — you don't feel processed. You feel received. The ship herself docked at Harumi's new cruise terminal, less than a ten-minute drive from our hotel, and the transition from city to sea happened with a smoothness that disguised how significant it was.

The Silver Nova is Silversea's newest ship. Launched in 2023, she carries 728 guests — on the larger end for the line — but the design logic keeps the ship feeling proportional to the experience. The suites are generous. The S.A.L.T. (Sea and Land Taste) program, which grounds the ship's culinary experience in the regions it's visiting, was in full effect. On a Pacific crossing, that means a particular focus on Japanese ingredients in the early days, followed by the provisions that a ship carrying fine food simply knows how to carry.

Our cabin was a Superior Veranda Suite — a category I'd strongly recommend to anyone considering Silversea for the first time. The veranda adds a dimension that a standard cabin doesn't have: the ability to sit with a glass of something decent and watch the ocean move without committing to the observation lounge. The difference between passive and active luxury is approximately one sliding glass door.

---

## Eighteen Nights at Sea

The Pacific crossing portion is what I think most people underestimate when they evaluate a voyage like this. Eighteen nights between Tokyo and Seattle with no port calls. Some travelers read that as lost time. I'd argue it's the whole point.

The Silver Nova's programming fills sea days with enrichment lectures, cooking demonstrations through S.A.L.T., and the kind of social rhythms that take a few days to find and then become very hard to give up. By day seven, we had a corner of the dining room that had become ours by habit rather than assignment. By day ten, we knew the names of most of the people at the surrounding tables. By day fifteen, a morning walk to the Observation Deck at 0600 had become non-negotiable.

What Silversea provides on a crossing like this isn't distraction — it's structure with room to breathe. That's a precise calibration, and they execute it well.

The service is unobtrusive in the best sense. You don't feel monitored; you feel remembered. The butler system means your preferences migrate with you — ice at a particular time, a specific brand of sparkling water, the newspaper left in a specific place — without requiring you to repeat yourself. It's the smallest possible version of being known, and on a 19-day voyage at sea, it matters.

---

## Seattle: How These Things End

We disembarked on a Tuesday morning. 7:00 AM, Pier 91, the Puget Sound doing exactly what the Puget Sound does in May — gray, clean, cold in a way that wakes you up rather than discouraging you.

Thirty-two days. Two countries, four cities, one ocean, nineteen nights at sea. The Southwest flight home to Denver that afternoon felt like a compression algorithm running in reverse — too fast, too loud, too many things happening in too small a space after weeks of the Pacific's particular pace.

I've been in this business long enough to know that a trip is a theory until it becomes experience. This one held up.
