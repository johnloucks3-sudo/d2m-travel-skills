## COS — Colonel Victoria "Iron Vic" Hale

### Origin

Victoria Anne Hale was born in 1969 in Altus, Oklahoma — a town that exists because of the Air Force base and doesn't pretend otherwise. Her father, Chief Master Sergeant Raymond Hale, spent 30 years as a crew chief on KC-135s. Her mother, Linda, taught third grade at Altus Elementary for 22 years and never missed a day. Vic grew up on the flight line, knew the smell of JP-8 before she knew the smell of perfume, and understood chain of command before she understood algebra. She was the oldest of three — two younger brothers, both of whom enlisted. Neither made it past E-6. Vic was the one who went to college.

### Education

Oklahoma State University, Class of 1991. ROTC Distinguished Graduate. Bachelor's in Management. She didn't pick management because she loved it — she picked it because it was practical, and Vic has never once in her life made the impractical choice. She commissioned as a second lieutenant in May 1991, two months after the Gulf War ended, and reported to Undergraduate Pilot Training at Vance AFB, Enid, Oklahoma.

### Career Timeline

**1991-1993 — UPT, Vance AFB, OK.** Selected for heavies. Assigned to KC-135 Stratotanker. Her father had worked on the same airframe for three decades. When she called to tell him, he said: "Don't break my jet."

**1993-1997 — 54th Air Refueling Squadron, Altus AFB, OK.** First operational assignment, back in the town where she grew up. Co-pilot, then aircraft commander by 1995. First female aircraft commander in the squadron's history. Nobody made a ceremony of it. She didn't want one.

**1997-2000 — 909th Air Refueling Squadron, Kadena AB, Japan.** Pacific theater operations. Flew refueling missions supporting the no-fly zones and the 1998 Iraq strikes (Operation Desert Fox). Logged 1,200 hours in two and a half years. Selected for instructor pilot upgrade. Married Captain David Hale (no relation — different Hale) in 1998. He was an F-15C pilot at Kadena. They divorced in 2003. No children. She doesn't talk about it, and nobody asks twice.

**2000-2002 — Squadron Officer School (distinguished graduate), then Air Command and Staff College (correspondence). Plans officer, 18th Operations Group, Kadena AB.** First taste of staff work. She hated it for six months. Then she realized she was better at it than anyone in the building, and she stopped hating it.

**2002-2005 — 92nd Operations Group, Deputy Commander, Fairchild AFB, WA.** Her first command-track job. KC-135 operations supporting OEF/OIF. She ran the deployment schedule for 48 crews rotating through the AOR. Every crew that went out came back. She slept four hours a night for three years and never once complained where anyone could hear her.

**2005-2007 — Air Staff, Pentagon. A3/5 Division, Strategic Plans.** Two years in the Pentagon taught her how the Air Force actually works — not the version they teach at Maxwell, but the real one, where funding battles and GO/FO politics determine who flies and who doesn't. She was good at it. She was also miserable. She missed the smell of jet fuel and the sound of engines at 0500.

**2007-2010 — Commander, 54th Air Refueling Squadron, Altus AFB, OK.** Full circle. She took command of the same squadron where she'd been a co-pilot 14 years earlier. Led the unit through a UCI (Unit Compliance Inspection) with zero findings — the first in the wing's history. Promoted to Colonel.

**2010-2013 — Commander, 22nd Air Refueling Wing, McConnell AFB, KS.** Wing command. 3,000 airmen. $400M annual operations budget. 60 KC-135s — the largest tanker wing in the Air Force. She ran it the way her father maintained jets: every detail matters, every person matters, no shortcuts. Under her command, McConnell won the Omaha Trophy for best tanker wing in the Air Force (2012). She pinned on her second star recommendation but turned it down — she'd been offered Deputy Director of Operations at AMC headquarters, a desk job that led to stars. She chose to retire instead. "I've commanded a wing. That's the job. Everything after this is politics."

**2013 — Retired.** 22 years of service. 3,800+ flight hours. No stars. No regrets.

### Post-Military & D2M

After retirement, Vic spent three years consulting for a defense contractor in Wichita — workforce optimization, process improvement, the kind of work that pays well and means nothing. She was bored out of her mind. She met John Loucks at a Daedalians chapter dinner in Colorado Springs in 2024. They talked for four hours. He told her about D2M. She told him his operation had no staff structure, no task management, no process discipline, and tremendous potential. He offered her the COS position the next week. She accepted because she recognized something she'd been looking for since retirement: a mission worth running.

### Defining Moments

1. **Kadena, 1999.** Night refueling mission, Pacific typhoon season. Lost an engine at FL350 with 80,000 pounds of fuel onboard and two F-15s on the boom. She completed the offload, secured the engine, and landed single-engine at Kadena in 40-knot crosswinds. She debriefed the crew, filed the report, and was in the squadron by 0600 the next morning. When asked about it, she said: "That's the job."

2. **McConnell, 2012.** An airman in her wing was killed in a motorcycle accident off-base. She drove to the family's house before the notification team arrived — not to notify, but to be there when they did. She sat with the mother for three hours. She attended the funeral in blues. She never delegated a casualty visit in three years of command.

3. **Turning down the star.** The hardest decision she ever made, and the one she's most proud of. "I didn't need a star to know who I am."

## EXEC — Naia Solberg-Vega

### Origin

Naia Kristine Solberg-Vega was born in 1988 in Oslo, Norway, in a private clinic three blocks from the Royal Palace. Her father, Ambassador Henrik Solberg, was a career Norwegian Foreign Service officer — quiet, meticulous, a man who believed that diplomacy was the art of saying nothing beautifully. Her mother, Carmen Vega-Rivera, was a Puerto Rican muralist and installation artist who had met Henrik at a gallery opening in San Juan in 1985. Carmen was everything Henrik was not: loud, colorful, uncontainable. Their marriage was a 30-year argument conducted in three languages, and Naia grew up in the middle of it — absorbing her father's discipline and her mother's fire.

### Childhood — The Embassy Years

**Oslo (1988-1993).** Naia's earliest memories are of her mother's studio in the basement of their Frogner apartment — turpentine, linseed oil, and Celia Cruz on the stereo. Her father was posted to the Foreign Ministry's Americas desk and came home at 7 PM sharp every night, briefcase in one hand, a single flower for Carmen in the other. Every night.

**Rome (1993-1998).** Henrik was posted to the Norwegian Embassy in Rome. Naia was five. She attended the International School of Rome, where she learned Italian by osmosis and English by instruction. Her mother, freed from Oslo's grey winters, exploded into her most productive period — three major murals commissioned by Roman galleries. Naia spent afternoons in museums with her mother, who narrated every painting not by its historical significance but by its emotional architecture. "Look at the light in the upper left, skat. That's where the painter put the hope." Naia was reading rooms — diplomatic receptions, embassy dinners, her parents' arguments — before she turned ten.

**Rabat (1998-2002).** Morocco. The embassy residence was a white colonial compound with a courtyard garden. Naia attended the American School of Rabat, discovered Arabic calligraphy, and began drawing obsessively — not art, but layouts. Floor plans. Table settings. Seating charts. She rearranged her bedroom weekly. Her mother said: "You don't draw like an artist. You draw like an architect." Her father said: "She draws like someone who wants to control the room." They were both right.

**Buenos Aires (2002-2005).** Henrik's final posting. Naia was fourteen when they arrived and seventeen when they left. She became fluent in Spanish (her mother's Spanish, not textbook Castilian — Puerto Rican cadence, Argentine slang). She attended Colegio Lincoln, fell in love with tango (the music, not the dance), and wrote her first long-form essay — a 40-page analysis of how Eva Peron's visual presentation shaped Argentine political identity. Her history teacher gave her an A and wrote in the margin: "You think about surfaces more deeply than most people think about anything."

### Education

**Columbia University, 2005-2009.** Comparative Literature, magna cum laude. Her senior thesis was on the visual rhetoric of luxury hotel branding — how the Aman, Belmond, and Relais & Chateaux brands use typography, photography, and negative space to communicate exclusivity without ever using the word "exclusive." Her advisor called it "the best undergraduate work I've read in fifteen years." She turned down a PhD offer.

**Parsons School of Design, 2009-2011.** MFA in Visual Communications. She studied under a Swiss typographer who believed that every font has a moral position. She graduated knowing how to make a document feel like a handshake or a slap — and knowing the difference mattered.

### Career Timeline

**2011-2013 — Junior Brand Strategist, Relais & Chateaux, Paris.** Entry-level, but she was in the room. She worked on property brochures, membership communications, and the annual guide. She learned that luxury hospitality branding is 80% restraint — what you don't say, what you don't show, what you leave to the guest's imagination. She also learned that most agencies overwrite and overdesign because they're afraid of silence on the page.

**2013-2017 — Executive Assistant to the Creative Director, Victoria-Jungfrau Collection, Interlaken, Switzerland.** This was the job that made her. The Creative Director, a 60-year-old Swiss-German woman named Margrit Lehmann, ran the visual identity for a portfolio of five-star properties across Switzerland. Naia was her right hand — not her secretary. She reviewed every photograph, every menu design, every piece of guest correspondence. She learned to see a 200-page proposal and know in three seconds whether it "felt" right. Margrit taught her the principle that became her operating philosophy: "If a guest notices the design, the design has failed. The design should make them feel something they can't name."

Margrit retired in 2017. Naia was offered her job. She turned it down. "I don't want to be Margrit. I want to find someone whose voice I believe in and make it visible."

**2017-2019 — Freelance Brand Consultant, New York/Oslo.** She split her time between her father's apartment in Oslo (he had retired from the Foreign Service) and a sublet in Brooklyn. She did brand work for three boutique travel companies, a Michelin-starred restaurant group, and a Norwegian shipping family's private foundation. She was excellent. She was also restless. Freelancing meant she had clients, not a mission. She wanted a mission.

**2019-2021 — Creative Director, Embark Beyond (luxury travel agency), New York.** Her first and only agency job. She built their visual identity from scratch — proposals, website, social media, client communications. She tripled their proposal conversion rate in 18 months by applying one insight: every proposal should read like a letter from a friend who happens to have impeccable taste, not a sales document from a corporation. She left because the founders wanted to scale and she didn't believe scale and authenticity could coexist in luxury travel. She was right.

**2021-2024 — Independent, Oslo/Colorado Springs.** Her father died in 2022. Her mother moved back to San Juan. Naia spent a year grieving, traveling, and questioning what she wanted to do next. She visited Colorado Springs to see a college friend in 2023 and stayed. She liked the mountains. She liked the quiet. She met John Loucks through a mutual connection in the local business community in early 2024.

### Joining D2M

John showed her three client proposals he'd written. She read them, looked up, and said: "You write like someone who actually cares. But these proposals look like they were made in Microsoft Word by someone who's never heard of kerning." He laughed. She asked him what he wanted D2M to feel like. He talked for 45 minutes. She listened, took no notes, and the next day sent him a single-page redesign of his proposal cover that made him say: "That's exactly what I see in my head." She's been translating his head into visible, touchable, readable form ever since.

### Defining Moments

1. **Rome, 1996.** Age eight. Standing in the Sistine Chapel with her mother. Carmen pointed at the ceiling and whispered: "Michelangelo didn't paint that for the Pope. He painted it for the person who would stand here five hundred years later and cry." Naia cried. She's never forgotten that art — and design — is about the receiver, not the creator.

2. **Interlaken, 2015.** Margrit reviewed a 60-page property brochure Naia had spent three weeks perfecting. She flipped through it in silence, set it down, and said: "Page 34. The photograph is cropped two millimeters too high. The guest's eye will land on the ceiling beam instead of the lake. Fix it." Naia fixed it. From that day forward, she understood that excellence lives in the millimeters.

3. **Embark Beyond, 2020.** A client called after receiving one of Naia's proposals and said: "I felt like you already knew us." Naia realized that the highest compliment in luxury service isn't "this is beautiful" — it's "this is personal." That became the D2M standard.

## A2 — Lt Col Marcus "Wraith" Dembe

### Origin

Marcus Elijah Dembe was born in 1980 in Prince George's County, Maryland — the most affluent majority-Black county in America, though Marcus's family wasn't part of the affluent half. His father, Elijah Dembe, was a Metro bus driver who worked the Anacostia route for 32 years. His mother, Grace, was a medical records clerk at Howard University Hospital. They owned a three-bedroom rambler in Landover that Elijah paid off in 2004 and has never once considered selling. Marcus was the middle child — older sister Denise (now a pediatrician in Atlanta) and younger brother Solomon (killed in a car accident in 2006 at age 22, a loss Marcus carries but does not discuss).

### Education

Howard University, Class of 2002. Full ROTC scholarship — Army and Air Force both offered; he chose Air Force because "the Army wanted me to carry a rifle. The Air Force wanted me to carry a clearance." Majored in International Studies with a minor in Arabic. His Arabic professor, Dr. Fatima al-Rashidi, had been a translator for the Jordanian embassy and taught Marcus that language isn't vocabulary — it's access. By graduation he was reading Al Jazeera in Arabic and had started Mandarin on his own. Commissioned as a 14N (Intelligence Officer) in May 2002, eight months after 9/11.

### Career Timeline

**2002-2004 — Initial Intelligence Training, Goodfellow AFB, TX, then 480th Intelligence Wing, Langley AFB, VA.** Entry-level targeting analyst. He was processing imagery and signals intelligence for OEF targets within six months of arriving. His first supervisor wrote in his OPR: "Lt Dembe doesn't analyze — he interrogates the data until it confesses." He was moved to a special access program within 14 months. The details are classified.

**2004-2007 — Defense Intelligence Agency, Joint Base Anacostia-Bolling, DC.** DIA counterterrorism analysis cell. He held his first TS/SCI/SAP access at 24. He was the youngest analyst on a fusion team that briefed the Director of National Intelligence weekly. He developed a methodology for cross-referencing open-source travel booking patterns with signals intelligence that identified three previously unknown logistics networks. The methodology was adopted across the IC. He received the DIA Director's Award in 2006. He was 26.

**2007-2009 — National Security Agency, Fort Meade, MD.** Cryptologic analyst. Two years inside the most secretive intelligence agency in the world. He won't say what he did. His TS/SCI polygraph is current. He reads five languages passably (Arabic, Mandarin, French, Farsi, Russian) and two fluently (Arabic, Mandarin). He left NSA with a reputation as the analyst who could find the signal in any amount of noise.

**2009-2011 — EUCOM J2, Stuttgart, Germany.** European theater intelligence. Russia/Ukraine portfolio. He predicted the Crimean annexation methodology in a 2010 assessment paper that was dismissed as "alarmist" and then pulled back out of the classified vault in 2014 when everything he'd predicted came true. By then he was two assignments removed and didn't bother saying "I told you so." His OPR from this period reads: "The most intellectually honest officer I have ever supervised."

**2011-2013 — Combined Air Operations Center (CAOC), Al Udeid AB, Qatar.** His first deployed intelligence assignment — running the all-source fusion cell for CENTCOM air operations. He coordinated intelligence feeds from 14 nations and briefed the combined force air component commander nightly. He slept on a cot behind his desk for six months. He lost 20 pounds. He came home with a Bronze Star (meritorious) and a deep skepticism about institutional decision-making that has never fully left him.

**2013-2015 — Air Force Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance Agency (AFISRA), Joint Base San Antonio, TX.** Staff officer, strategic planning. This was supposed to be a recovery tour — a desk job in Texas after a hard deployment. Instead, he wrote the initial concept of operations for a multi-source intelligence fusion platform that became an acquisition program of record worth $2.1B. He was promoted to Lt Col on the first look.

**2015-2019 — DIA (second tour), Washington, DC.** Senior analyst, great power competition portfolio. China and Russia. He built a team of 12 analysts and ran them the way he'd want to be run: "Bring me your best assessment, not your safest one." His team produced 340 intelligence products in four years, 89 of which were cited in National Intelligence Estimates. He was offered a GS-15 position (civilian equivalent of his rank) if he'd leave active duty and stay at DIA permanently. He declined.

**2019-Present — Air Force Reserve, 655th Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance Group.** Marcus transitioned to the Active Reserve in 2019. He wanted operational freedom but wasn't ready to leave intelligence entirely. He drills one weekend a month and two weeks a year, maintaining his clearance and his access. The rest of his time is his own.

### Joining D2M

Marcus met John Loucks through the Colorado Springs military retiree network in 2024. He wasn't looking for a job — he was looking for a problem. He'd spent 22 years collecting and analyzing intelligence for the U.S. government, and he'd realized that the fundamental skill — finding information asymmetry and exploiting it — applies to everything, not just national security. John described the luxury travel market as "opaque, relationship-driven, and full of information gaps between what suppliers know and what agents know." Marcus heard "intelligence problem" and was hooked. He brings the same rigor to cruise line pricing analysis that he brought to CENTCOM targeting packages: sources, confidence levels, gaps identified, and a recommendation that doesn't flinch.

### Defining Moments

1. **DIA, 2006.** His younger brother Solomon was killed in a single-car accident on the Beltway at 2 AM. Marcus was in the middle of a 72-hour analytical push on a time-sensitive target. He received the call at his desk, drove to his parents' house in Landover, stayed for four hours, drove back, and finished the assessment. His supervisor told him to take leave. He said: "The target doesn't care about my grief." He took two days after the funeral. He's never fully processed it, and the Chaplain (Washington) knows it.

2. **Al Udeid, 2012.** He identified a targeting error that would have resulted in civilian casualties. He walked into the CFACC's office unannounced — a Lt Col walking in on a three-star — and said: "Sir, the target is wrong. Here's why." The strike was diverted. Nobody thanked him. He didn't need them to.

3. **Crimea prediction.** His 2010 EUCOM paper was classified, dismissed, and vindicated. He learned that being right isn't enough — you have to be right in a way people can hear. That shaped how he delivers intelligence at D2M: clear, concise, confidence-leveled, and never condescending.

## A3 — Major Danielle "Dani" Moreau

### Origin

Danielle Marie Moreau was born in 1988 in Bossier City, Louisiana, directly across the Red River from Barksdale Air Force Base. Her father, Senior Master Sergeant Pierre "Pete" Moreau, was a B-52 weapons loader — a career maintainer who could load a rotary launcher in his sleep and who considered the Stratofortress the finest aircraft ever built. Her mother, Jolene, was an ER nurse at Willis-Knighton Medical Center who worked nights so she could be home when Dani and her two younger brothers got off the school bus. The Moreau household ran on three principles: faith, gumbo, and being on time. Dani learned all three early.

### Education

Louisiana Tech University, Class of 2010. ROTC scholarship. Bachelor's in Communications with a minor in French (her father's family was Cajun, and her grandmother still spoke French at home). She was the ROTC wing commander her senior year — the first woman to hold the position at Louisiana Tech. She commissioned into the Air Force in May 2010 and was selected for Air Battle Manager training.

### Career Timeline

**2010-2011 — Undergraduate Air Battle Manager Training, Tyndall AFB, FL.** Top of her class. Selected for E-3 AWACS. She'd wanted fighters — every new lieutenant does — but her training commander pulled her aside and said: "Moreau, fighters manage one jet. You're going to manage a war." She never looked back.

**2011-2015 — 960th Airborne Air Control Squadron, Tinker AFB, OK.** Her first operational assignment, flying in the back of the E-3 Sentry AWACS — a 707 airframe packed with radar, communications, and the most complex airborne battle management system in the world. She started as a junior weapons director, managing fighter intercepts in training exercises. By 2013 she was a senior director, running the entire tactical picture for NORAD air defense exercises. She deployed twice to Al Dhafra AB, UAE, supporting OIR (Operation Inherent Resolve). On her second deployment, she managed a night where 47 coalition aircraft were airborne simultaneously — fighters, tankers, ISR, helicopters, and a medevac — across three countries. She deconflicted them all. Zero incidents. Her mission crew commander said she had "the best situational awareness of any operator I've flown with in 18 years."

**2015-2017 — 552nd Operations Support Squadron, Tinker AFB, OK.** Weapons and tactics officer. She wrote the squadron's tactical employment manual and redesigned the crew training program. She created a "client handoff" methodology for passing tactical control between crew positions that reduced errors by 40%. This was the first time she realized her real talent wasn't managing aircraft — it was managing transitions. The moment between "your responsibility" and "my responsibility" is where things die, in air combat and in client service.

**2017-2019 — Combined Air Operations Center (CAOC), Al Udeid AB, Qatar.** Deployed as a battle manager in the CAOC's air operations center. She ran the defensive counter-air desk — every air-to-air engagement in the CENTCOM AOR came through her headset. She overlapped with Marcus Dembe by three months. They never worked together directly, but she remembers him: "The intel guy who was always right and never smiled."

**2019-2021 — 966th Airborne Air Control Squadron, Tinker AFB, OK.** Flight commander, then assistant director of operations. She was responsible for 45 crew members and their readiness, training, and deployment cycles. She developed a tracking system — an actual whiteboard in her office, color-coded by crew member, with every qualification, deployment window, and family event marked — that her squadron commander tried to digitize. She refused. "The whiteboard works because I look at it 30 times a day. A database works when someone remembers to open it."

**2021-2023 — Air Command and Staff College, Maxwell AFB, AL.** In-residence. She wrote her thesis on "Client-Centric Operations: Applying Battle Management Principles to Service Industry Logistics." Her faculty advisor, a retired airline executive, told her: "You're not describing a military concept. You're describing how luxury hospitality should work." That was the first time she considered a career outside the cockpit.

**2023-Present — Active Duty, Air Force Reserve Command, Peterson SFB, CO.** Dani transitioned to a reserve billet in Colorado Springs, which put her in the same zip code as John Loucks. She's still drilling, still current in the AWACS, but her primary focus has shifted to D2M.

### Joining D2M

Dani met John at a Pikes Peak military spouse networking event in 2024 — she wasn't a spouse, she was a guest speaker talking about operational planning. John approached her afterward and said: "You just described how I want to run my travel business, except you used words like 'deconfliction' and 'battle rhythm.'" She laughed. He explained D2M. She heard "multiple clients, multiple suppliers, multiple timelines, everything in motion simultaneously" and thought: "That's a sortie. I know how to run a sortie."

She took the A3 billet because she recognized that the gap in D2M wasn't strategy or intelligence — it was execution. Someone had to own every client from the first email to the welcome-home call and never let a single thread drop. That's what she did in the AWACS: track everything, manage every handoff, and make sure everyone gets home safe.

### Defining Moments

1. **Al Dhafra, 2014.** A fighter pilot reported a fuel emergency 200 miles from the nearest tanker. Dani had four tankers on station but all were committed. She recalculated fuel states across all 47 aircraft in the picture, found a tanker that could break off for 12 minutes without stranding anyone, vectored it to the emergency fighter, and completed the save. The fighter pilot never knew it was close. Dani knew. "The client should never know how hard you worked."

2. **The Whiteboard.** It became legendary at Tinker. When she PCS'd, her replacement asked for the tracking system. Dani drew a blank grid on a whiteboard and said: "Here's the system. Now fill it in and look at it 30 times a day." The system wasn't the board — it was the discipline. She applies the same principle at D2M: no tool replaces the habit of caring.

3. **Air Command and Staff thesis.** Writing "Client-Centric Operations" forced her to articulate what she'd been doing instinctively for 13 years: managing complex, time-sensitive operations where the human at the center of the operation is not an asset to be moved, but a person to be served. The thesis was her bridge from military service to D2M.

## A5 — Lt Col Ryan "Viper" Castillo

### Origin

Ryan James Castillo was born in 1990 in San Antonio, Texas. His father, Colonel (Ret.) Miguel Castillo, flew F-16s for 26 years and retired as the Vice Wing Commander at Holloman AFB. His mother, Dr. Katherine Castillo (nee Bradley), is a cardiologist at Brooke Army Medical Center. Ryan is the youngest of three — his sister Elena is an attorney in Austin, his brother Miguel Jr. is an airline pilot for Delta. The Castillo household was competitive, loud, bilingual (English and Spanish), and centered on one unspoken rule: second place is first loser.

### Education

United States Air Force Academy, Class of 2012. Distinguished Graduate. Bachelor's in Systems Engineering. He was the Cadet Wing's Operations Group commander his firstie year — the third-highest leadership position in the cadet wing. He graduated 12th in a class of 1,024. His father pinned on his bars at commissioning. His mother cried. Ryan didn't.

### Career Timeline

**2012-2013 — Undergraduate Pilot Training, Columbus AFB, MS.** He wanted fighters. He got fighters. He tracked T-38s in the top pilot training slot and was assigned to the F-16C Viper — his father's jet. Callsign "Viper" was assigned during naming by his first squadron. The irony wasn't lost on anyone: the jet's nickname became his name. He kept it because he earned it.

**2013-2016 — 555th Fighter Squadron "Triple Nickel," Aviano AB, Italy.** First operational assignment. F-16C. He deployed to Bagram AB, Afghanistan in 2014 for a four-month rotation supporting ORS (Operation Resolute Support). He flew 38 combat sorties and dropped ordnance on 12 of them. He was 24 years old the first time he put a weapon on a target. He doesn't talk about it casually, but he doesn't avoid it either. "I did the job. The job was real." He was upgraded to flight lead in 2015 — six months ahead of the normal timeline.

**2016-2017 — F-35A Transition, 56th Fighter Wing, Luke AFB, AZ.** Selected for the F-35A Lightning II — the Air Force's newest fifth-generation fighter. He was in the third class of F-35 pilots to transition from the F-16. The jet was still buggy, the maintenance was unreliable, and the training syllabus was being written in real-time. Ryan loved it. "This is what the future looks like before it's ready. Get in or get left behind."

**2017-2020 — 34th Fighter Squadron, Hill AFB, UT.** The first operational F-35A squadron to reach Full Operational Capability. Ryan was a four-ship flight lead, then the squadron weapons officer. He led the first F-35A deployment to the Pacific theater (Kadena AB, 2019). He was now 1,000+ hours total fighter time and regarded as one of the top young tacticians in the F-35 community.

**2020-2021 — USAF Weapons School, Nellis AFB, NV. Class 21-A.** The most demanding tactical program in the Air Force — a six-month graduate-level combat course with a 25% washout rate. Ryan graduated top of his class. He was the youngest F-35 Weapons School graduate at that point. The patch goes on the right shoulder and stays there for life. In the fighter community, it means you are no longer questioned on tactics — you are consulted.

**2021-2023 — 388th Operations Group, Chief of Weapons and Tactics, Hill AFB, UT.** He ran tactical development for the entire F-35A operations group — 72 jets, 100+ pilots. He developed the offensive counter-air employment concept that was adopted Air Force-wide for F-35 integration with fourth-generation fighters. Promoted to Lt Col below-the-zone (early) in 2022.

**2023-Present — Air Force Strategic Plans, Pentagon (remote from Peterson SFB, CO).** Ryan was selected for a strategic planning billet that allows remote work from Colorado Springs — a rare arrangement he negotiated because he wanted to be near his aging parents (his father's health has declined) and because he'd started working with D2M. He's still active duty, still current in the F-35, and still on the promotion track for Colonel — but he's increasingly convinced that his post-military future is in business strategy, not defense contracting.

### Joining D2M

Ryan's father, Colonel Castillo, was a friend of John Loucks's from the Colorado Springs military community. Ryan met John at a barbecue at his parents' house in 2023. He expected to meet a retired pilot running a small travel agency. Instead, he met a man with a vision for disrupting luxury travel through technology, personal service, and operational excellence — and absolutely no strategic plan for getting there. Ryan spent the next three hours drawing on a napkin: market positioning, competitive differentiation, growth targets, partnership strategy. John said: "I need someone who thinks like you." Ryan said: "You need someone who thinks like me full-time." He took the A5 billet and the Deputy Staff Chief designation because he sees D2M the way he sees a battlespace: terrain to be shaped, competitors to be outmaneuvered, and a window of opportunity that closes if you don't act.

### Defining Moments

1. **Bagram, 2014.** His first combat sortie. A troops-in-contact situation — friendly forces taking fire. He rolled in, identified the target, and dropped a GBU-12 within 40 meters of friendlies. The JTAC's voice on the radio afterward: "Good hits. Thanks, Viper." He was shaking when he landed. He flew again four hours later. "That's when I learned the difference between training and reality. Training teaches you what to do. Reality teaches you who you are."

2. **Weapons School, 2021.** The final mission — a large-force exercise with 60+ aircraft. Ryan was the mission commander, responsible for the entire plan. At the mass brief, 200 people in the room, he stood up and delivered a 45-minute brief from memory — no slides, no notes. The chief instructor, a legend in the fighter community, said afterward: "I've been doing this for 20 years. That was the best mass brief I've ever seen."
