# 32 Days, One Ocean, Zero Shortcuts: Tokyo to Seattle on the Silver Nova

There's a particular silence that happens somewhere around day five at sea. No horizon but water. The ship's engines a low, constant hum beneath you. The only thing that moves is the bow cutting northeast across the Pacific. That silence is why I do this.

This past April, my wife Susan and I spent 32 days traveling from Colorado Springs to Tokyo, across the North Pacific, through Alaska's Inside Passage, and home from Seattle on Silversea's Silver Nova. Friends and colleagues who've heard about it tend to respond with some version of the same question: *How do you plan something like that?* The honest answer is: the same way you'd plan anything worth doing — one decision at a time, with enough patience to get the details right.

This is the story of how it went.

---

## The Build-Up: Orange County and Five Nights in Waikiki

The trip started well before Tokyo. We flew to Orange County first — a family visit, a reset before the real travel began. Three nights at the Newport Beach Marriott Bayview, time with our son. The logistics of a 32-day international journey have their own gravitational pull. You spend weeks tracking confirmations, rebooking what the airline changed, monitoring the weather at ports you've never visited. Those three days in Orange County were the last stretch of genuine stillness before everything started moving.

Then Hawaii.

If you've never stopped in Honolulu on the way to Japan, I'd argue you're doing it wrong. The Hale Koa Hotel in Waikiki is a military resort — available to active duty, retirees, and veterans — and it stands up to any civilian property on the island at a fraction of the price. Five nights, ocean view, and a stretch of the same beach that the major resorts are charging four times as much to access. We ate well, walked far, and watched the sun drop into the water every evening without once checking what time it was back home. That's a Honolulu calibration.

---

## Business Class to Tokyo: What JAL Gets Right

The Japan Airlines HNL–HND flight is one of the most underrated business class products in the Pacific. The Sky Suite III cabin is a full lie-flat private suite with a sliding privacy door and a window that faces you properly. No sliding past a stranger to get to your seat. The food is Japanese before it's airline food — a distinction that matters at 30,000 feet over the open ocean. We landed in Tokyo before dawn, cleared customs in forty minutes, and were in our room at the Hilton Tokyo Odaiba before breakfast.

Tokyo gets covered extensively in travel media, and deservedly so. What I'll say is this: the city rewards specificity. The morning we took the shinkansen to Kyoto and hired a guide named Hiro to walk us through the Nishiki Market — stopping at a tofu shop that had been operating since the 1870s, a ramen counter that seated eight people and had a 45-minute wait — that morning was not available on any standard tour. The day after, an early bus tour to the Mt. Fuji viewpoint at Owakudani, and the clouds parted at exactly the right moment. Sometimes the tour is right.

---

## Boarding the Silver Nova at Harumi Port

The embarkation at Tokyo Harumi is quieter than you'd expect for a ship of Silver Nova's scale. Silversea keeps its terminal operations unhurried — you don't feel processed. You feel received. The Silver Nova docked at Harumi's new cruise terminal, a short drive from our hotel in Odaiba, and the transition from city to sea happened with a smoothness that disguised how significant it was.

The Silver Nova is Silversea's newest ship. Launched in 2023, she carries 728 guests — on the larger end for the line — but the design logic keeps the ship feeling proportional to the experience. The suites are generous. The S.A.L.T. (Sea and Land Taste) program, which grounds the ship's culinary experience in the regions it's visiting, was in full effect. In Japanese waters, that means an early focus on Japanese ingredients that carries into the Pacific crossing with a particular clarity.

Our cabin was a Superior Veranda Suite — a category I'd strongly recommend to anyone considering Silversea for the first time. The veranda adds a dimension that a standard cabin doesn't have: the ability to sit with a glass of something decent and watch the ocean move without committing to the observation lounge. The difference between passive and active luxury is approximately one sliding glass door.

---

## Nineteen Days: Japan, the Pacific, and Alaska

The voyage structure is what I think most people underestimate when they evaluate a crossing like this. Our first port after Tokyo was Miyako, Iwate — a coastal city in northern Honshu, two days out of Tokyo. We went ashore for the Jodogahama coastline and the Ryusendo cave, which is one of the three largest limestone caves in Japan and almost entirely absent from international travel coverage. That morning established the tone for the rest of the voyage.

Then the crossing began.

From Miyako, Silver Nova turned northeast into open Pacific. Nine days of open water — the kind of passage that either works for you or doesn't, and I've learned over the years to be honest with clients about which category they fall into. For us, it works. The sea day programming is thoughtful — enrichment lectures, S.A.L.T. cooking demonstrations, the social rhythms that take a few days to establish and then become very hard to give up. By day seven, we had a corner of the dining room that had become ours by habit rather than assignment. By day ten, we knew the names of most of the people at the surrounding tables. A 0600 walk to the Observation Deck had become non-negotiable.

What Silversea provides at sea isn't distraction — it's structure with room to breathe. They execute that calibration well.

Then Alaska.

The Inside Passage is the reason I keep recommending the Tokyo-to-Seattle itinerary over any other Pacific crossing. Sitka came first — we did the culinary adventure tour, which is worth the morning. Juneau followed, then Wrangell, then Ketchikan. Each of these ports has its own character. Wrangell is small enough that you feel like you're actually visiting a place rather than arriving at a destination. Ketchikan's creek street and the fishing boats are exactly what they look like in photographs and more interesting for it. The night before Seattle, we stopped in Victoria for a morning tour by horse-drawn trolley — a city that handles the contrast between its colonial architecture and its position on the Pacific coast with more grace than I expected.

The service across all nineteen days was unobtrusive in the best sense. You don't feel monitored; you feel remembered. The butler system means your preferences migrate with you without requiring you to repeat yourself. It's the smallest possible version of being known, and on a passage of this length, it matters considerably.

---

## Seattle: How These Things End

We disembarked on a Tuesday morning. Pier 91, the Puget Sound doing exactly what the Puget Sound does in May — gray, clean, cold in a way that wakes you up rather than discouraging you.

Thirty-two days. Three countries, a dozen ports, one ocean, and a crossing that started in the Pacific and ended in a November-colored Seattle morning. The Southwest flight home to Denver that afternoon felt like a compression algorithm running in reverse — too fast, too loud, too many things happening in too small a space after weeks of the Pacific's particular pace.

I've been in this business long enough to know that a trip is a theory until it becomes experience. This one held up.
