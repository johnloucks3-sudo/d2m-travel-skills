# Why We Always Stop in Hawaii on the Way to Japan

The direct route from Colorado Springs to Tokyo runs through Denver or Los Angeles and clocks in around eleven hours in the air. It's fine. It works. I've done it. But every time Susan and I head to Japan for a Pacific cruise — and we've done it twice now — we route through Honolulu first. Not a connection. Not an overnight. Five nights.

People who hear this tend to think we're padding the vacation. We're not. We're building a better trip.

Here's why.

---

## The Geography Argument

Look at a Pacific map. The continental US to Tokyo is roughly 5,700 miles. Los Angeles to Honolulu is about 2,560 miles. Honolulu to Tokyo is another 3,800. You're not adding meaningful distance — you're breaking the journey at a natural midpoint that happens to be one of the most beautiful places on earth.

The jet lag math also changes. Flying direct to Tokyo puts you close to eleven hours of time adjustment behind. Flying to Honolulu first moves you six hours (mountain time to Hawaii). Then Honolulu to Tokyo moves you five hours more — but across two separate adjustments, with five days of Hawaii living in between to recalibrate. You arrive in Tokyo feeling like yourself.

That's not a small thing when you're about to board a ship for 32 days.

---

## The Hale Koa

The Hale Koa Hotel in Waikiki is a military resort on a 72-acre oceanfront property managed by the Armed Forces Recreation Centers. It is available to active duty military, retirees, veterans, and their families. If you qualify, and you're routing through Hawaii on the way to a Pacific cruise, it is — without any exaggeration — one of the best-kept secrets in American travel.

The property sits on the same stretch of Waikiki beach as the major resort hotels. The Hilton, the Sheraton, the Royal Hawaiian — they're all neighbors. The beach is identical. The sunset is the same. The access to the water, the restaurants, the shops, the experience of being in Waikiki — all of it is available to Hale Koa guests the same as it is to guests paying $600–900/night at the properties next door.

The Hale Koa rate depends on rank and availability, but for a retired officer it typically runs $100–200/night. For a retired colonel, the ocean view rooms are genuinely stunning — not dated-military-facility stunning, but actually good.

We've stayed in the Tower Building, ocean side, high floor. The morning light on the water is something I try not to take for granted.

---

## Five Nights Is Not Too Long

This is where people push back. Five nights in Honolulu before a 32-day cruise feels like it's adding a second vacation before the vacation.

I'd argue the opposite. The cruise is compressed. Every day brings a new port, a new time zone approximation, a new set of decisions. The days feel long and then, at the end, short. Five nights in Waikiki is the only stretch of the entire trip where there is genuinely nothing you have to do.

We walk. We eat. We sit at the beach bar and watch the surf schools operate in the shallower water past the reef. We find one good dinner each evening — there's a Japanese restaurant near the Hyatt that Susan has requested every trip, and at this point I'd be in trouble if I suggested skipping it. We read. We sleep until we're no longer jet-lagged.

By the time we board the Japan Airlines flight from HNL to HND, we're rested in a way that a red-eye connection through LAX would never produce.

---

## What Honolulu Actually Is

This is a short note, because it's important: Honolulu is not just a layover destination. It's a destination.

People who've never spent real time there tend to think of it as generic beach resort — interchangeable with Cancún or Cabo. It isn't. The food culture alone — Japanese, Korean, Filipino, native Hawaiian, Portuguese — is distinct in a way that rewards paying attention. The history is substantial and close to the surface. Pearl Harbor. The Bishop Museum. The campus of the University of Hawaii at Mānoa, where the Pacific Studies program has done some of the finest documentary work on Polynesian navigation and migration.

You don't have to visit any of that. But the city has layers that reward people who stay long enough to find them.

---

## The Practical Case

If you're planning a Japan cruise — Silversea out of Tokyo, Princess out of Yokohama, any embarkation from the Japanese port cities — I'll make the routing argument directly:

**Book the Honolulu stop.** Five nights minimum. Hale Koa if you qualify; Outrigger Reef or Waikiki Beach Marriott if you don't. Set an arrivals email to your hotel and a departures email to your ship. Leave everything else open.

The trip you remember won't start at embarkation. It'll start the morning you watch the sun come up over Diamond Head from a tower room in Waikiki, coffee in hand, with nothing to decide and nowhere to be.

That's why we always stop.
