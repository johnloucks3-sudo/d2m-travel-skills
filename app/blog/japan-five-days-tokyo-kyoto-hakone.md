# Four Nights in Tokyo Before Your Cruise: What to Skip, What to Keep, and Where to Eat

Most people flying to Tokyo for a cruise embarkation treat the city as a logistical waypoint — arrive, recover from jet lag, board the ship. That's a mistake.

Tokyo rewards staying put. Not rushing. Not trying to see everything. The travelers I've watched do Japan well arrive with a shorter list than they think they need and a longer timeline than they think they can afford. Four nights is right for a pre-cruise Japan stay. Two is not enough. A week is ideal if you can manage it.

This past April, Susan and I spent four nights in Tokyo before boarding the Silver Nova at Harumi Port. What follows is the honest version of what we planned, what we actually did, and what I'd change.

---

## The Logic of Four Nights

The trap Japan sets for American travelers is the belief that Tokyo and Kyoto are a day trip apart. They are, by shinkansen — about two and a half hours on the bullet train. But that framing flattens what makes both cities worth visiting. Tokyo is a horizontal city of neighborhoods, each with its own distinct personality. Kyoto is a vertical city of layers — historical, spiritual, culinary — that compounds with time. A day in Kyoto is just enough to understand why you need more.

Our four nights in Tokyo broke down like this:

- **Night 1 (Apr 19):** Arrive Tokyo. Recover. The overnight JAL flight from Honolulu lands early morning. You're not sightseeing Day 1 — you're getting horizontal.
- **Day 2 (Apr 20):** Tokyo — Tsukiji Outer Market, Shinjuku, Shibuya
- **Day 3 (Apr 21):** Kyoto day trip — Nishiki Market, Fushimi Inari approach, dinner
- **Day 4 (Apr 22):** Mt. Fuji & Hakone bus tour, back to Tokyo by evening
- **Day 5 (Apr 23):** Embarkation morning — private transfer to Harumi Port

That's not the tourist itinerary. It's the one that worked.

---

## Getting the Hotel Right

We stayed at the Hilton Tokyo Odaiba for all four nights. The conventional wisdom pushes visitors toward Shinjuku or Marunouchi — fine choices both — but Odaiba has an underappreciated advantage for pre-cruise arrivals: the Harumi Port cruise terminal is a direct, ten-minute drive across the Odaiba bridge. On embarkation morning, we loaded into our private minibus transfer and watched Tokyo's skyline recede from the bay. No navigating central Tokyo traffic with luggage.

The Odaiba waterfront is also quieter than central Tokyo. That matters on arrival night, when your body still thinks it's afternoon in Colorado and the city's energy is more overstimulating than it should be. The bay view, the slower pace, the Ferris wheel lit against the water — it's an easier landing than dropping into the center of Shibuya jet-lagged off an overnight flight.

## Tokyo: One Full Day, Neighborhood Logic

**Day 2** should be about walking, not monuments. We started at Tsukiji Outer Market at 8 AM — the inner wholesale market has moved to Toyosu, but the outer market is still operational, still extraordinary, and still one of the better introductions to Tokyo I know. Tamagoyaki. A tuna rice bowl that could ruin you for sushi at home. Sea urchin for people who've been skeptical of sea urchin. Eat slowly.

Shinjuku in the afternoon, specifically the area around the Golden Gai — a grid of tiny bars, each seating six to ten people, that survive in a city otherwise committed to scale. Don't try to do everything in Shinjuku. Pick one bar. Stay long enough to have a conversation.

Shibuya crossing for fifteen minutes before dinner. Not an hour — fifteen minutes is enough to understand it and move on.

## The Day in Kyoto: Hire Hiro

This is the most specific advice I have, and I'm going to give it specifically.

Don't do Kyoto on your own for a one-day visit. Hire a guide. Not a tour group — a guide. One person, contracted for the full day, who knows the city the way a local knows their own kitchen.

We booked ours in advance — a guide named Hiro, found through a food tour booking service. He met us at Kyoto Station when we arrived from Tokyo on the shinkansen, and by the time we'd walked five minutes from the station, he was explaining the history of Kyoto's relationship with its own food culture in a way that no guidebook had managed to do.

The Nishiki Market is four blocks long and compressed enough that first-time visitors tend to walk through it quickly. Hiro stopped at seven different stalls. At a tofu shop that had been operating since the 1870s, a woman in her seventies handed us cubes of freshly made tofu still warm from the press. At a pickled vegetable stand, Hiro talked with the owner in rapid Japanese and then translated a five-minute explanation of the particular fermentation process they'd been using for sixty years. That's not available on the standard tour.

In the afternoon, the approach to Fushimi Inari — the thousand-gates path — before the main tourist wave arrived. At dinner, Hiro took us to a ramen counter in a covered arcade that seated eight people, had a 45-minute wait, and served a tonkotsu broth that I am still thinking about.

A private food guide for a day in Kyoto runs approximately $200–350 USD depending on the individual and what's included. It is the highest-ROI line item in any Japan itinerary I have seen.

## Hakone: When the Mountain Is and Isn't There

The standard Hakone debate among Japan travelers: organized bus tour vs. self-navigated Romancecar train journey.

I'll give you the honest answer: it depends on the day.

If the weather is clear, take the Romancecar from Shinjuku, book a room near Hakone-Yumoto, and navigate independently. The mountain will reveal itself at the right time if you're patient and positioned correctly.

If the weather is uncertain — which it was for us in late April — the organized bus tour makes more sense than it has any right to. Our tour departed Tokyo at 7:50 AM, delivered us to the Hakone Open Air Museum mid-morning (which turned out to be genuinely worth three hours), and reached the Mt. Fuji viewpoint at Owakudani at precisely the moment the clouds parted. We had Fuji in full, unobstructed silence for about twenty minutes. Then the clouds returned.

I have no idea how the tour operator managed that timing. I suspect they didn't — I suspect we got lucky, and organized tours give you more opportunities to get lucky than independent navigation does when conditions are marginal.

## The Practical Notes

**Currency:** Japan is still significantly cash-based. Exchange at the airport or at a 7-Eleven ATM (they accept foreign cards reliably). Having ¥50,000 in cash on arrival is not excessive for a four-night visit.

**Transport:** The IC card (Suica or Pasmo) loads onto Apple Wallet now. Load ¥5,000 when you arrive and it covers every subway, bus, and convenience store purchase you'll need.

**Language:** Google Translate's camera mode, pointed at a menu or a sign, is close enough to functional that it changes the experience substantially. Use it.

**Luggage:** Japan's luggage forwarding services (Yamato Transport's "takkyubin") let you send bags directly from your hotel to your ship in advance. We had our larger bags waiting in the cabin when we boarded. The morning of embarkation, we were traveling light.

## Before You Board

The embarkation at Tokyo Harumi is quieter than you'd expect for a ship of Silver Nova's scale. Silversea keeps its terminal operations unhurried — you don't feel processed. But the transition from the city to the ship still has a particular weight to it that I'd encourage you to feel rather than rush through.

When you step onto the Silver Nova's gangway and the port recedes behind you, Japan is the last thing you'll have seen. If you've spent those four nights correctly — slowly, curiously, with enough space to actually notice where you are — that departure has the right gravity.

That gravity is what turns a voyage into a memory worth keeping.
