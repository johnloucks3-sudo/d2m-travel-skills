# Four Nights in Japan Before Your Pacific Cruise: What We Actually Did

*By John A. Loucks III, CEO, Dreams2Memories Travel, LLC*

---

We had four nights in Japan before boarding Silver Nova in Yokohama. This was our second visit to Tokyo. We knew the basics. We thought we were prepared for Kyoto. Here is what actually happened — the good, the demanding, and the one meal I'm still thinking about.

---

## Arrival: Haneda to Odaiba

JAL Flight 73 from Honolulu lands at Haneda in the early morning. By the time we cleared customs, collected luggage, sorted ground transportation, and got to the hotel, it was close to 6 PM. The day evaporated. This is normal on a westbound Pacific crossing — you land early and somehow arrive late — and the lesson is not to plan anything ambitious for day one. We checked in and that was enough.

We stayed at the Hilton Odaiba. This requires explanation because most Tokyo visitors base in Shinjuku or Shibuya, and Odaiba is a deliberate choice in the other direction.

Odaiba is a reclaimed island in Tokyo Bay, southeast of the city center. We chose it because it's newer, the views of the bay and the Rainbow Bridge are genuinely good, and it felt less chaotic than the main entertainment districts after a long international flight. The Hilton property there is a solid hotel — well-maintained, comfortable rooms, views that delivered on the promise.

Two things to know: the Executive Dining Room is worth it. Breakfast options, Japanese and American, at a level that justifies the upgrade. The regular hotel breakfast, on the other hand, is a cattle call — crowded buffet, slow service, the kind of thing you push through rather than enjoy. If you're staying at the Hilton Odaiba, eat upstairs.

---

## Kyoto: Beautiful, Demanding, and Honest

I want to be honest about Kyoto because the way it gets written about and the way it is on the ground are two different things.

We took the Shinkansen from Tokyo. The bullet train is genuinely easy — fast, smooth, straightforward to board if you have a Japan Rail Pass or know what you're doing with the ticket gates. Our guide met us on the platform as we stepped off. That is the mark of a well-organized tour: you don't wander through a station looking for a sign with your name on it. She was there when we arrived.

The guide was excellent. Genuinely knowledgeable about Kyoto culture, unhurried, made the history feel present rather than recited. The temples and neighborhoods she chose were worth every minute we spent at them.

Here is the part I want you to hear clearly if you're 70 or older, or if mobility is a consideration for you or your travel companion: **Kyoto is a walking city and it leans hard on its bus system.** We did not know in advance how bus-dependent the day would be. We logged over 9,000 steps. For two people in their seventies with some mobility challenges, that is a significant physical day. Not impossible — we did it — but we were tired in a way that was more than just pleasant fatigue.

If this is relevant to your situation, tell your travel advisor before the tour is booked, not after. A good operator can redesign the day to reduce walking, arrange private transport between stops, and pace the itinerary differently. Ours was wonderful, but the logistics weren't designed for our specific needs and we absorbed the gap. You don't have to.

The food highlight of Kyoto: Yuba. It's a tofu preparation — specifically the skin that forms on the surface of heated soy milk, served in a light broth in a way that's somewhere between fondue and Japanese kaiseki. Delicate, subtle, deeply satisfying. I didn't know what I was ordering and it turned out to be one of the better things I ate on the entire trip. If you're doing a food tour in Kyoto, ask about yuba.

## Hakone and Mt. Fuji: Adjust Your Expectations

The Hakone Loop is the standard framework for seeing Mt. Fuji from the Fuji Five Lakes region: drive up, take the ropeway over the volcanic zone, get the mountain views, have lunch, drive back through winding mountain roads.

Here is what I can tell you from doing it.

The ropeway — a cable car that crosses over active sulfur vents — is an experience, but I wouldn't call it a highlight. The volcanic landscape below is stark and striking in a geological sense, but it's not the scenic payoff the brochure implies. If you have a fear of heights or enclosed spaces in open-air gondolas, skip it without guilt. If you're going for the mountain views, there are better vantage points.

Mt. Fuji itself: here's the honest thing nobody tells you. When you're close to it, it loses the iconic quality. The perfect cone, the snow cap, the symmetry — those are things you see from a distance. Get too close and you're looking up at what feels like a high-altitude terrain feature rather than the image you've had in your head since childhood. Mt. Fuji is a photograph and a symbol and a real mountain. The real mountain looks best from twenty miles away.

What was genuinely excellent: lunch. We stopped at a restaurant — traditional architecture, garden setting, a koi pond visible from the dining room — and the buffet was the kind of Japanese country cooking that you can't get in Tokyo. Simple ingredients, prepared carefully, the kind of meal that doesn't announce itself and then surprises you. The setting alone was worth the drive.

The Hakone roads are long and winding. If you're susceptible to motion sickness, medicate in advance.

---

## Four Nights: Was It Enough?

This was our second time in Tokyo. Four nights was right for a second visit.

If it's your first time, I'd give it five nights minimum and be honest about what you can actually absorb. Tokyo is overwhelming in the best way, and trying to compress the first-time experience into four days leaves you with a list of things you didn't get to rather than the things you actually did. The city rewards time and repeated visits.

For a pre-cruise itinerary, the structure we used worked: two days Tokyo base, one day Kyoto, one day Hakone/Fuji, board the ship. What I'd change for travelers with mobility considerations is building more ground transportation into the Kyoto day and setting that expectation before departure, not at the station.

The four nights in Japan were a strong beginning to what became nineteen days on Silver Nova. Japan calibrated us. It slowed us down in the right way. It reminded us that travel isn't about covering distance — it's about arriving somewhere and actually being there.
