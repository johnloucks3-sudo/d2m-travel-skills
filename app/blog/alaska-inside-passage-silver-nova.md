# Five Ports in Four Days: Alaska's Inside Passage on Silver Nova

*By John A. Loucks III, CEO, Dreams2Memories Travel, LLC*

---

The Inside Passage doesn't ease you in. By the time *Silver Nova* cleared open water and the first glaciated peaks came into view, I understood why people come back to Alaska over and over. We had just spent nineteen days at sea — Japan behind us, Seattle ahead — and Alaska was the final act. It did not disappoint.

We hit five ports in four days: Sitka on May 5th, Juneau on May 6th, Wrangell on May 7th, Ketchikan on May 8th, and Victoria on May 10th before disembarking in Seattle on the 11th. Here is what I can tell you about each one, honestly.

---

## Sitka: The Forgotten Capital

Most people skip Sitka. The itineraries that list it often schedule it as the first Alaska stop after days at sea, which means guests are still in open-ocean mode — sea day mentality, slightly unfocused. That's a mistake.

Sitka was the original capital of Russian Alaska. The cathedral, St. Michael's, sits right at the center of town and is worth an hour of your morning. The Alaska Raptor Center is a short walk and genuinely impressive — they rehab bald eagles and other raptors here, and you'll see birds up close in a way you can't anywhere else.

The town is small enough to walk, which is the correct way to do Sitka. Don't book a bus tour. Walk from the tender pier, turn toward the cathedral, and let the town unspool in front of you. We had rain — which Alaska gives you whether you want it or not — and it was still one of the better mornings of the trip.

*Silver Nova* anchors and tenders here. The tender operation is smooth and well-managed. If you have mobility concerns, speak with the ship's team early — they will make arrangements.

---

## Juneau: Capital Without Roads

Juneau is Alaska's capital and has no road connection to the rest of the state. The only way in is by water or air. That geographic oddity turns out to produce one of the more interesting port towns on the Inside Passage.

The Mendenhall Glacier is the anchor attraction and for good reason. It has retreated dramatically in recent decades — you can see the waterline on the rock where it once stood — and the combination of beauty and urgency makes it worth the trip every time. Book ahead. The good tours fill up months out.

Downtown Juneau is compact and walkable. The Red Dog Saloon gets a lot of visitor traffic and is exactly what you expect; take it or leave it. What I'd recommend instead: walk up the hill behind the governor's mansion. The views over the channel are exceptional.

Juneau gets a lot of cruise ships. In summer, the dock can feel crowded. *Silver Nova* docks at the main terminal, which puts you within walking distance of most of the town. The difference between an ultra-luxury ship and a larger vessel is visible here — the crowd ratios at every excursion are dramatically different.

---

## Wrangell: The Port You've Never Heard Of

Wrangell is the sleeper. This is the small town that larger itineraries skip entirely, which means the guests who end up here often find it the most memorable stop.

It was once a hub for gold rush prospectors — the Stikine River runs nearby and was a primary route into the interior — and the town still has that frontier-adjacent quality. Fewer than 2,500 people live here. The harbor is small. The docks are quiet.

What Wrangell has that nowhere else on the Inside Passage has: Petroglyph Beach. It sounds like a tourist name, but it's genuine. There are ancient rock carvings right on the beach, accessible at low tide, with no interpretive center, no ticket booth, no rope barriers. You walk out and crouch down to look at faces and spirals carved into the rock by people who were here thousands of years before the Russian traders or the gold seekers.

I had low expectations for Wrangell and left with it near the top of my list.

---

## Ketchikan: Salmon, Rain, and Totem Poles

Ketchikan calls itself the "Salmon Capital of the World," which is the kind of thing you'd dismiss as chamber-of-commerce copy except that it's basically accurate. Salmon here is as fresh and abundant as anywhere I've been.

The town is long and narrow, built on a hillside above a waterway. The main street runs along the water. The totem poles at Totem Bight State Historical Site are the real thing — carved and maintained by Tlingit and Haida artists — and the park is quiet enough that you can actually look at them without being swept along by tour groups.

Creek Street is the famous historic district, built on pilings above Ketchikan Creek. It was a red-light district in the gold rush era, which the town is happy to tell you about. Today it's shops and restaurants, but the architecture and the setting on the water make it genuinely pleasant.

If you're a fly fisherman, Ketchikan is worth planning a day around. Charter boats operate out of the harbor and the runs are serious.

---

## Victoria: The Elegant Landing

Victoria isn't Alaska — it's British Columbia — but it's the final port before Seattle and it earns its place on the itinerary.

The Empress Hotel fronts the inner harbor and is one of those buildings that looks exactly like you imagined it would before you arrived. Tea at the Empress is a legitimate experience if you care about that sort of thing; it's been running since 1908. The Butchart Gardens, about 20 minutes from the harbor, are extraordinary — 55 acres of curated gardens on a former limestone quarry.

Victoria is polished in a way that Alaska isn't, and after four days in small port towns it's an interesting contrast. The harbor is busy with floatplanes and foot ferries. The walkable waterfront is well maintained. It's a comfortable last stop before Seattle.

---

## The Inside Passage Itself

What you're really booking when you book this itinerary is not the ports — it's the passage between them. The Inside Passage runs between the mainland and a chain of islands, and the channel narrows in places to a few hundred yards. *Silver Nova* moves through it at a pace that lets you stand on the open decks and watch the tree line pass.

I've sailed a lot of itineraries. The Inside Passage at dawn, with mist still sitting on the water and the mountains on both sides, is in a category of its own.

We had nineteen days on *Silver Nova* and the Alaska segment was four days of them. But those four days are what I think about most.
